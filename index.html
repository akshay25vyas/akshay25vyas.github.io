I build ways to measure and certify what machine learning systems do.

I'm a PhD candidate in Computer Science at The University of Texas at Dallas, advised by Nicholas Ruozzi and working closely with Vibhav Gogate, graduating in May 2027. I work on two connected problems: evaluation that tracks what people actually notice, and learned inference that keeps formal guarantees.

In my first-author AAAI 2026 paper, we showed that pixel and n-gram metrics barely move when models are trained to respect task invariants, while most Fréchet distances on learned features improve. I'm now comparing what human raters notice with what automatic judges notice, from metrics to LLM judges and reward models. My first-author NeurIPS 2026 Spotlight introduced Neural Dual Bounds, to our knowledge the first learned MAP bound predictor that is valid by construction for any network output.

Before the PhD I spent nearly three years building production systems at Swiggy, Goldman Sachs and Microsoft, and I bring that engineering to research. I integrated a neural bound predictor into a C++ branch-and-bound solver that queries it at every search node with millisecond latency, and I built perception components for an AR assistant that our team demonstrated at MIT under DARPA's Perceptually-enabled Task Guidance program.

AAAI 2026First author

A Novel Approach to Evaluating Evaluation Metrics for Multi-Output Structured Prediction

Akshay Vyas, Angelo Pimienta, Nicholas Ruozzi

Uses approximate task invariants to test which evaluation metrics register the difference between models trained with and without them, across colorization, translation and captioning.

AAAI 2026: 17.6% acceptance (4,167 of 23,680 submissions).

Paper Code

In multi-output structured prediction tasks, while only one ground truth label may be provided in the training data, multiple equally valid outputs may be possible, making reliable evaluation a persistent challenge. We postulate that human evaluators implicitly use task-specific invariants, e.g., object boundaries in colorized images or named entities in translations, to judge if an output is acceptable. Under this assumption, we introduce a notion of approximate task-specific invariants and use them as diagnostic tools to evaluate a variety of existing metrics for vision and language tasks. We use these task invariants as part of a framework to systematically test metric reliability by encouraging domain-relevant invariants in model outputs via an augmented loss function. In our experiments, we observe that enforcing invariants with an augmented loss yields substantial improvements in popular distributional metrics while more traditional metrics change only marginally. Through this invariants-driven evaluation, we expose where standard metrics fail to detect meaningful differences, and we highlight the conditions under which distributional metrics succeed or still fall short.

CaptainCook4D: A Dataset for Understanding Errors in Procedural Activities

Rohith Peddi, Shivvrat Arya, Bharath Challa, Likhitha Pallapothula, Akshay Vyas, Bhavya Gouripeddi, Qifan Zhang, Jikai Wang, Vasundhara Komaragiri, Eric Ragan, Nicholas Ruozzi, Yu Xiang, Vibhav Gogate

384 egocentric recordings (94.5 hours) of people cooking from recipes, some following the steps and some deliberately making errors, benchmarked for error recognition, multi-step localization and procedure learning.

NeurIPS 2024 Datasets and Benchmarks: 25.3% acceptance (460 of 1,820 submissions).

Paper arXiv Project Annotations

Following step-by-step procedures is an essential component of various activities carried out by individuals in their daily lives. These procedures serve as a guiding framework that helps to achieve goals efficiently, whether it is assembling furniture or preparing a recipe. However, the complexity and duration of procedural activities inherently increase the likelihood of making errors. Understanding such procedural activities from a sequence of frames is a challenging task that demands an accurate interpretation of visual information and the ability to reason about the structure of the activity. To this end, we collect a new egocentric 4D dataset CaptainCook4D comprising 384 recordings (94.5 hours) of people performing recipes in real kitchen environments. This dataset consists of two distinct types of activities: one in which participants adhere to the provided recipe instructions and another in which they deviate and induce errors. We provide 5.3K step annotations and 10K fine-grained action annotations and benchmark the dataset for the following tasks: error recognition, multi-step localization and procedure learning.

Research

Two questions run through my work. How do we know an output is good when many outputs are acceptable and a metric sees only one reference? And how do we make learned shortcuts for inference safe enough to run inside exact solvers?

Evaluation that tracks what people notice

Colorization, translation and captioning each admit many acceptable outputs, yet most metrics score against a single reference. In our AAAI 2026 paper we used approximate task invariants, such as object boundaries, depth, named entities and scene-graph relations, as probes. We retrained seven published models across three tasks with and without invariant losses computed by frozen networks, so no new labels were needed, and measured which metrics register the difference.

Most Fréchet distances on learned features improved, by as much as 33%, while PSNR, BLEU-4 and METEOR moved by less than 2%. The results suggest building metrics on richer encoders, such as DINOv2 rather than Inception.

Learned inference with guarantees

Exact solvers such as branch-and-bound query an upper bound at every node of the search. Classical bounds are always correct but slow to tighten. Learned predictors are fast, yet one bound that comes in too low can prune away the optimal solution.

How it works

The model has three binary variables split into three overlapping pieces. A message λ on a shared variable is added to one piece and subtracted from its neighbor, so every complete assignment keeps its total score. Maximizing each piece on its own can only overshoot, which makes the sum of piece maxima, D(λ), an upper bound on the optimum for any λ the network outputs.

Coordinate descent on the same messages closes this model's gap from 1.2 to 0 in three updates. Every prediction above is the optimal solution plus Gaussian noise with standard deviation 0.25 on each output.

Neural Dual Bounds

NeurIPS 2026 Spotlight

The network outputs antisymmetric edge messages on a join graph, so every output, whether trained, untrained or adversarial, yields a valid upper bound on the MAP value. For MAP it trains self-supervised against a smooth dual objective, with no LP solver and no optimal labels. On UAI benchmark models its warm starts cut JGLP's dual gap by up to six orders of magnitude within 100 iterations, and test-time adaptation extends the method to MAP under multiple constraints.

Learning to Bound

Under review

A network that is monotone by construction, combined with stratified conformal calibration, gives fast heuristics for AND/OR branch-and-bound. On Bayesian networks its bounds are 5 to 10 times tighter than mini-bucket bounds at i-bound 4 and arrive in milliseconds. Along search paths it records zero ordering violations, where an unconstrained network inverts roughly one step in four.
